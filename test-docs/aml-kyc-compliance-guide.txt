Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Know Your Customer (KYC) Compliance Guide

Section 1: Regulatory Framework

1.1 Overview of AML Regulations
Anti-money laundering regulations are designed to prevent the financial system from being used for money laundering and terrorist financing. Financial institutions must comply with a comprehensive set of regulations including:

- Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) - requires financial institutions to assist government agencies in detecting and preventing money laundering
- USA PATRIOT Act - enhanced due diligence requirements for correspondent accounts and private banking
- Financial Action Task Force (FATF) Recommendations - international standards for combating money laundering
- EU Anti-Money Laundering Directives (AMLD) - European framework for AML compliance
- Taiwan Money Laundering Control Act - domestic regulations for financial institutions operating in Taiwan

1.2 Regulatory Bodies
Key regulatory bodies overseeing AML compliance include:
- Financial Supervisory Commission (FSC) in Taiwan
- Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) in the US
- Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) in the UK
- FATF as the global standard-setting body

Section 2: Know Your Customer (KYC) Requirements

2.1 Customer Identification Program (CIP)
All financial institutions must implement a CIP that includes:
- Collection of identifying information (name, date of birth, address, identification number)
- Verification of identity through documentary or non-documentary methods
- Record-keeping of identification information and verification methods
- Comparison against government lists of known or suspected terrorists

2.2 Customer Due Diligence (CDD)
CDD involves understanding the nature and purpose of customer relationships:
- Identifying and verifying the identity of customers
- Identifying and verifying beneficial owners (individuals owning 25% or more)
- Understanding the nature and purpose of the customer relationship
- Ongoing monitoring of customer transactions

2.3 Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD)
EDD is required for higher-risk customers and situations:
- Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs) and their family members
- Customers from high-risk jurisdictions
- Correspondent banking relationships
- Private banking clients
- Non-face-to-face business relationships
- Unusual or complex transaction patterns

Section 3: Transaction Monitoring

3.1 Suspicious Activity Reporting
Financial institutions must file Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) when they detect:
- Transactions that involve funds derived from illegal activities
- Transactions designed to evade reporting requirements
- Transactions with no apparent lawful purpose
- Patterns of transactions that suggest money laundering or terrorist financing

3.2 Currency Transaction Reports (CTRs)
CTRs must be filed for cash transactions exceeding $10,000 (or equivalent):
- Single transactions above the threshold
- Multiple related transactions (structuring detection)
- Both deposits and withdrawals

3.3 Red Flags for Money Laundering
Common red flags that should trigger enhanced scrutiny:
- Frequent large cash deposits followed by immediate wire transfers
- Transactions inconsistent with the customer's known business or personal activities
- Multiple accounts used to collect and funnel funds to a single account
- Reluctance to provide identification or complete documentation
- Unusual patterns of transactions just below reporting thresholds (structuring)
- Use of shell companies or nominees to obscure beneficial ownership
- Transactions involving high-risk jurisdictions without clear business purpose

Section 4: Record Keeping and Training

4.1 Record Retention Requirements
- CIP records: 5 years after account closure
- SAR records: 5 years from filing date
- CTR records: 5 years from date of transaction
- CDD documentation: duration of relationship plus 5 years

4.2 Employee Training
All employees must receive AML training covering:
- Recognition of suspicious activities
- Internal reporting procedures
- Regulatory requirements and consequences of non-compliance
- Role-specific training based on job function
- Annual refresher training

4.3 Independent Testing
AML programs must be independently tested:
- Annual testing by internal audit or external party
- Assessment of program effectiveness
- Testing of transaction monitoring systems
- Review of SAR filing decisions
- Evaluation of training program adequacy

Section 5: Penalties for Non-Compliance

5.1 Criminal Penalties
- Fines up to $500,000 per violation
- Imprisonment up to 10 years for willful violations
- Enhanced penalties for violations related to terrorist financing

5.2 Civil Penalties
- Fines up to $25,000 per day for each violation
- Cease and desist orders
- Removal of officers and directors
- Loss of banking charter or license

5.3 Reputational Risk
- Negative media coverage and public perception
- Loss of correspondent banking relationships
- Decreased customer confidence
- Regulatory scrutiny and enhanced examination frequency
